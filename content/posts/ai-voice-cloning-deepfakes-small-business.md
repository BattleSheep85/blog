A finance director at a UK company got a video call from his CFO. The CFO told him to authorize a $25 million transfer. The face, the voice, the mannerisms were all right. It was a deepfake. The money was gone.

That was a large company, but the technology that made it possible is cheap and getting cheaper. Small businesses are already seeing AI-powered attacks in their inboxes, and voice cloning and deepfake video are next.

<!--more-->

## AI phishing is already here

By October 2025, AI-generated phishing had become the number one email threat to businesses of all sizes. This isn't hypothetical. KnowBe4 found that 82.6% of phishing emails they analyzed contained AI-generated content.

That matters because AI eliminated the things we used to rely on to spot phishing. Bad grammar, weird formatting, awkward phrasing. Those red flags are gone. AI writes clean, professional emails that match the tone and vocabulary of real business communications. It can mimic the writing style of a specific person if the attacker has even a small sample to work with.

The result is phishing emails that are harder to distinguish from legitimate messages. Not slightly harder. Significantly harder. Your employees who were pretty good at catching phishing a couple years ago are now facing emails that look as professional as the real thing.

## Voice cloning is real and it's easy

You only need about three seconds of someone's voice to create a usable clone. Three seconds. A voicemail greeting, a conference call recording, a social media video. That's all an attacker needs to generate a voice that sounds convincingly like your CEO, your business partner, or your accountant.

Deepfake vishing (voice phishing) surged 1,600% in Q1 2025 alone. The attacks typically follow a pattern: an employee gets a phone call that sounds like their boss, urgently requesting a wire transfer, a password reset, or access credentials. The urgency and the familiar voice override the employee's normal skepticism.

Small businesses are particularly vulnerable here because people know each other's voices. In a 15-person office, a call that sounds like the owner carries a lot of weight. There's no security team to escalate to. There's just the person who answered the phone trying to decide if their boss really needs that transfer done right now.

## Video deepfakes are getting there

The $25 million case I mentioned wasn't a one-off. Real-time deepfake video is improving fast. Tools that can overlay someone's face and voice during a live video call exist today. They're not perfect yet, but they're good enough to fool people in a brief video meeting, especially with a shaky internet connection where occasional glitches seem normal.

For small businesses, the most realistic scenario right now is a short video call (not a two-hour meeting) where an attacker impersonates a known contact to authorize a specific action. Think of a five-minute Teams call from your "bank representative" or a quick Zoom check-in from your "supplier" about updated payment details.

## What makes small businesses vulnerable

**No verification culture.** When the boss calls and says do something, people do it. There's no formal process for verifying requests over a certain dollar amount, and adding one feels like you're saying you don't trust people. You're not. You're protecting them.

**Public voice samples everywhere.** Company YouTube videos, podcast appearances, conference presentations, voicemail greetings, even TikTok content. Every one of these is training data for a voice clone.

**Small teams with broad access.** The office manager who handles payroll, AP, and HR has the access to do a lot of damage when deceived. In a larger company, authorization is distributed and layered. In a small office, one person often has the keys to everything.

## How to defend against this

### Establish verification procedures

Any request involving money, credentials, or access changes needs to be verified through a separate channel. If you get a phone call asking for a wire transfer, hang up and call back on a known number. If you get a video call requesting something unusual, follow up with an email to the verified address. Make this a written policy, not a suggestion.

### Create a safe word or challenge phrase

This sounds low-tech because it is. Agree on a word or phrase that gets used to verify identity for sensitive requests. If someone calls asking for a transfer and can't provide the challenge phrase, the request gets verified through another method before anyone acts on it.

### Limit what's publicly available

You don't need to scrub everything, but think about what's out there. Does your CEO's voicemail greeting need to be a full 30-second message, or would "You've reached Chris, leave a message" work just as well? Every bit of audio you reduce makes voice cloning harder.

### Train for the new reality

Your phishing training needs to include AI-generated examples now. Show your team what a well-crafted AI phishing email looks like. Demonstrate voice cloning with a tool (there are legitimate ones like ElevenLabs that show how easy it is). When people see how realistic these fakes are, they take verification procedures seriously.

### Use email authentication

SPF, DKIM, and DMARC don't stop voice cloning, but they make email-based impersonation of your domain much harder. When every channel is a potential attack vector, closing the ones you can is worth doing.

### Be suspicious of urgency

Nearly every one of these attacks relies on urgency. "Do this now." "Don't tell anyone." "I'm in a meeting and can't talk, just handle it." Real business can almost always wait 10 minutes for verification. If someone is pressuring you not to verify, that's the biggest red flag there is.
