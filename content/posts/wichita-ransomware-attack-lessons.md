On May 5, 2024, the LockBit ransomware group hit the City of Wichita's computer systems and encrypted basically everything. Police officers went back to writing reports on paper. City hall could only accept cash payments. The airport's WiFi went down. For months afterward, the city was in recovery mode, and the full scope of the damage kept getting worse.

If you run a business in Wichita, you heard about this. You probably thought "that's rough" and went back to your day. But here's the thing: if LockBit can take down an entire city government with a dedicated IT department and a real budget, what do you think they could do to your 15-person shop?

<!--more-->

## What actually happened

LockBit, a Russian-linked ransomware operation, got into the city's network and deployed encryption across critical systems. The attack knocked out online bill payments, court systems, transit services, and internal city operations. Wichita PD couldn't access digital records. The city had to shut down large parts of its network just to contain the spread.

Then the data breach numbers came in. Over 77,000 police records were compromised, including names, Social Security numbers, and driver's license numbers. That's real people in our community whose personal information is now floating around on the dark web.

A Russian national was eventually indicted in connection with LockBit operations. But that doesn't un-encrypt the files or un-breach the data. Recovery took months, cost millions, and disrupted services for hundreds of thousands of residents.

## Why this matters to your business

You might think "I'm not a government. Nobody's targeting me." That's exactly the wrong takeaway. Ransomware gangs don't care how big you are. They care how easy you are. Automated scanners probe millions of IP addresses looking for unpatched systems, open RDP ports, and weak passwords. If your business is on the internet (it is), you're a target.

Here's what a ransomware hit looks like for a small Wichita business:

- **Day 1:** You can't open any files. Your accounting software won't launch. Your email is gone. There's a ransom note demanding $50,000 to $500,000 in Bitcoin.
- **Day 2-5:** You're calling every IT person you know. Your employees are sitting around unable to work. Customers are calling and you have no answers.
- **Week 2:** You find out your backups were on the same network and got encrypted too. The ransom just became your only option, and paying doesn't guarantee you get your data back.
- **Month 2:** You're still rebuilding. Some data is gone forever. A few customers left. You're out $100K+ between lost revenue, recovery costs, and new equipment.

This isn't hypothetical. This is what happens to small businesses every single day. 60% of small businesses that suffer a major cyberattack close within six months.

## The basics that would have helped

I'm not going to pretend there's a magic product that makes you ransomware-proof. There isn't. But most small business ransomware attacks succeed because of a handful of preventable failures:

**Backups that actually work.** Your backups need to be offsite, offline (or immutable), and tested regularly. "We back up to a USB drive" doesn't count if that drive is plugged in 24/7. A proper 3-2-1 backup strategy (three copies, two different media, one offsite) is the single most important thing you can do.

**Patching.** The city's attack exploited known vulnerabilities. Every month Microsoft releases security patches, and every month thousands of businesses ignore them. Set up automatic updates or pay someone to manage them for you.

**Multi-factor authentication (MFA).** If your email, VPN, or remote desktop only requires a password, you're one phishing email away from a breach. MFA is free on most platforms and stops the vast majority of credential-based attacks.

**Email filtering and training.** Most ransomware gets in through phishing emails. A good email filter catches the obvious ones. Training your staff to spot the rest is cheap and effective.

**Network segmentation.** If one computer gets infected, can it reach every other computer and server on your network? For most small businesses, the answer is yes, because everything is on one flat network. Segmenting your network limits how far an attack can spread.

## What Wichita businesses should do differently

The city's attack wasn't some exotic, nation-state-level operation. LockBit is a ransomware-as-a-service operation. They sell their tools to affiliates who do the actual hacking. The barrier to entry is low, and the targets are everywhere.

If you're a Wichita business owner, here's your action list:

1. **Get a vulnerability assessment.** Find out where your actual weaknesses are before someone else does.
2. **Fix your backup situation.** Cloud backup with immutable snapshots. Test restores quarterly.
3. **Turn on MFA everywhere.** Email, banking, remote access, everything.
4. **Get endpoint protection.** Not just antivirus. Modern endpoint detection and response (EDR) that can catch ransomware behavior in real time.
5. **Have an incident response plan.** Who do you call? What do you shut down first? Where are your critical documents? Figure this out before you need it.
