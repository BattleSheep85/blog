"Is it time to replace these computers?" is one of the most common questions I get from small business owners. The answer is almost never "replace everything right now." It's usually a mix: some machines need to go, some need a cheap upgrade, and some are fine for another few years. The trick is knowing which is which.

Here's how I evaluate office PCs and the budgeting approach that keeps you from facing a $15,000 surprise.

<!--more-->

## General replacement timelines

These aren't hard rules, but they're based on 20 years of watching hardware age in office environments:

**Desktop PCs: 5-8 years.** Desktops tend to last longer because they have better airflow, bigger power supplies, and don't get bounced around. A decent business desktop (Dell OptiPlex, HP ProDesk, Lenovo ThinkCentre) bought in 2021 should serve you well through 2026-2029.

**Laptops: 4-5 years.** Laptops take more abuse. Hinges wear out, batteries degrade, and they accumulate physical damage from being carried around. A business laptop bought in 2022 is approaching replacement territory in 2026-2027.

**Monitors: 8-12 years.** Modern LCD monitors last forever unless they physically break. Don't replace monitors just because they're old. Replace them when they die or when you need a different size or resolution.

**Peripherals (keyboard, mouse): replace when broken.** Don't budget for these on a cycle. Buy a few spares, keep them in a drawer, and swap them when something fails. Budget $50-100/year for a 15-person office.

## The warning signs a PC needs replacement

Age alone doesn't kill a PC. Here's what actually tells you it's time:

- **It can't run Windows 11.** If the hardware doesn't support TPM 2.0 and you can't enable it in BIOS, the machine is on borrowed time. (See my Windows 10 end-of-life post for details.)
- **Boot time is over 2 minutes.** Assuming you've already done basic maintenance (more on that below), a 2+ minute boot time means the hardware can't keep up.
- **Applications freeze or crash regularly.** Not a software problem, not a malware problem, just hardware that's too slow for current software demands.
- **Hardware failures are accumulating.** One part failure is normal. When you're replacing the power supply, then the RAM, then the fan, the machine is telling you something.
- **It's out of warranty and mission-critical.** A $600 PC that's 5 years old and out of warranty is not worth a $200 repair. Replace it.

## The SSD upgrade trick

Here's the single best upgrade you can make to an aging PC: replace the mechanical hard drive with a solid-state drive (SSD).

**The numbers:**
- A 500GB SATA SSD costs $40-60
- A 1TB SATA SSD costs $60-80
- Installation takes 30-60 minutes per machine (including cloning the existing drive)

**What it does:**
- Boot time drops from 2-3 minutes to 15-30 seconds
- Applications open instantly instead of bouncing in the taskbar for 30 seconds
- The entire machine feels like a new PC

**When it makes sense:**
The PC has a mechanical hard drive (HDD), it meets Windows 11 requirements (TPM 2.0), and the rest of the hardware is in good shape. Spending $60 and an hour of labor buys you 2-3 more productive years from that machine. That's an incredible return on investment compared to a $600-800 replacement.

**When it doesn't make sense:**
The PC already has an SSD and it's still slow. The PC doesn't meet Windows 11 requirements. The PC has other hardware issues (failing motherboard, expanding battery on a laptop, broken screen). In those cases, the SSD won't save it.

I'd estimate that 30-40% of the "slow computers" I'm called to look at are solved by an SSD swap and a RAM upgrade. No new hardware needed.

## RAM: the other cheap upgrade

If the PC has 4GB of RAM, it's going to struggle with modern Windows and browser-based work. Chrome alone can eat 4GB if you have enough tabs open.

- 8GB is the minimum for office work in 2026
- 16GB is ideal for anyone who runs multiple applications simultaneously
- RAM upgrades cost $20-40 for desktops, $25-50 for laptops

Combined with an SSD, a RAM upgrade can transform a sluggish 4-year-old PC into something that feels new. Total cost: $80-130 in parts.

## The staggered replacement budget

The worst thing you can do is run every PC until it dies and then replace your entire fleet at once. A 15-person office replacing everything at the same time is looking at $9,000-15,000 in hardware, plus the IT labor to set it all up. That's a painful check to write.

Instead, stagger your replacements:

**Replace 20-25% of your fleet each year.** For a 15-person office, that's 3-4 PCs per year. At $600-800 per desktop or $800-1,200 per laptop, that's $2,400-4,800 per year. Predictable, budgetable, and you never face the "everything is old at the same time" cliff.

**Prioritize by role and condition:**
1. Replace the oldest machines first
2. Give the newest machines to the heaviest users (designers, accountants, developers)
3. Cascade older-but-functional machines to lighter users (reception, warehouse, conference rooms)

**Annual budget guideline for a 15-person office:**
- 3-4 PC replacements: $2,400-4,800
- 2-3 SSD/RAM upgrades on mid-age machines: $200-400
- Spare peripherals: $50-100
- Total: roughly $2,650-5,300/year

That works out to about $175-350 per employee per year for hardware, which is a reasonable line item in your IT budget.

## When to buy refurbished

Refurbished business-class PCs (off-lease Dell, HP, Lenovo) can be a great deal:

- A 3-year-old Dell OptiPlex refurb: $200-350
- A 3-year-old Lenovo ThinkPad refurb: $300-500
- Usually comes with a 1-year warranty

These make great machines for light-duty roles: reception, conference room displays, basic office tasks. I wouldn't put a power user on a refurb, but for the front desk that runs Outlook and a web browser, it's hard to beat.

**Where to buy:** Dell Refurbished, Amazon Renewed (look for sellers with high ratings), or local IT asset disposition companies.
