If you run a small business and think you're too small to be a ransomware target, I need you to hear this: you're not too small. You're the perfect size. Ransomware gangs have figured out that small businesses pay faster, fight less, and have fewer defenses than the big corporations everyone reads about in the news.

I've been doing IT consulting for 20 years, and the shift over the last few years has been dramatic. These aren't random attacks anymore. They're organized, efficient, and they're pointed straight at businesses like yours.

<!--more-->

## The numbers are ugly

Ransomware attacks increased 34% in 2025. In the US alone, there were over 5,010 incidents, a 50% jump from the prior year. Two-thirds of those attacks targeted organizations with fewer than 500 employees. And here's the stat that keeps me up at night: 88% of data breaches at small and mid-size businesses involved ransomware.

This isn't a big-company problem that occasionally trickles down. Small businesses are the primary target.

## Why small businesses specifically

It comes down to economics. Ransomware is a business, and small businesses are the most profitable segment.

**You have just enough to steal, but not enough to defend.** A 30-person accounting firm has client financial records, Social Security numbers, tax returns. That data is valuable. But that same firm probably doesn't have a security operations center, a dedicated IT team, or even basic endpoint detection. The attackers know this.

**You're more likely to pay.** A hospital system might have the resources to rebuild from backups and ride out weeks of downtime. A small manufacturer or law firm usually can't. When the choice is "pay $120,000 or close the business," most people pay. And 69% of businesses that paid the ransom got attacked again within a year. The gangs keep lists of who pays.

**You're the entry point to bigger targets.** A lot of small businesses are vendors or service providers for larger companies. Hack the 15-person HVAC contractor, use their credentials to get into the hospital network. This is exactly how the Target breach happened years ago, and the playbook hasn't changed.

## What it actually costs

The average ransomware payment for small businesses ranges from about $120,000 to $1.24 million, depending on who's counting and what they include. But the ransom payment is just the start. Factor in downtime (which averages weeks, not days), lost business, recovery costs, legal fees, potential regulatory fines, and the cost of telling all your customers their data was stolen.

For a lot of small businesses, a ransomware attack is a survival event. Not a bad week.

## The gangs operating right now

These aren't anonymous hackers in basements. They're organized groups with names, branding, and customer service departments (yes, really). The most active ones hitting small businesses in 2025 and into 2026 include:

**Akira** targets small and medium businesses specifically, often through VPN vulnerabilities. They go after healthcare, manufacturing, and professional services.

**Play** (also called PlayCrypt) focuses on IT, transportation, and construction companies. They typically get in through exposed RDP and FortiOS vulnerabilities.

**Qilin** has been averaging about 75 victims per month. They run a ransomware-as-a-service operation, meaning they lease their tools to affiliates who do the actual attacking.

**LockBit** was disrupted by law enforcement in 2024 but came back as LockBit 5.0. They remain one of the most prolific operations globally.

**RansomHub** emerged as a major player in late 2024 and continued through 2025, targeting a wide range of industries.

## What you can actually do

I'm not going to tell you to "implement a comprehensive cybersecurity framework." That's consultant-speak for "give me a lot of money." Here's what actually moves the needle for a small business:

**Turn on MFA everywhere.** Multi-factor authentication on email, VPN, cloud apps, everything. This alone blocks the majority of initial access attempts. It's free on Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace.

**Get real backups.** Not a USB drive plugged into the server. Cloud backups with separate credentials, at least 30 days of retention, and a tested restore process. If ransomware can reach your backups, you don't have backups.

**Patch your stuff.** Most ransomware gets in through known vulnerabilities that have had patches available for months. Set up automatic updates. If you have a firewall or VPN appliance, make sure its firmware is current.

**Get endpoint protection.** Windows Defender has improved, but a proper EDR solution like Microsoft Defender for Business ($3/user/month) or SentinelOne gives you actual detection and response capabilities.

**Have a plan.** A simple one-page incident response plan that says who to call, what to disconnect, and how to contact your insurance carrier. You don't need a 100-page playbook. You need to not be making decisions for the first time during a crisis.
