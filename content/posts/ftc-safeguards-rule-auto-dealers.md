If you run an auto dealership in Wichita, you're a financial institution in the eyes of the FTC. That's not a typo. Under the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act, any business that's "significantly engaged" in financial activities is considered a financial institution. That includes auto dealers who arrange financing, tax preparers, accountants, mortgage brokers, payday lenders, and real estate settlement companies.

The FTC's updated Safeguards Rule went fully into effect in 2023, with additional reporting requirements added in May 2024. If you haven't built a comprehensive cybersecurity program yet, you're already behind, and the fines are steep enough to make it a business-threatening problem.

<!--more-->

## What the Safeguards Rule requires

The updated Safeguards Rule isn't a vague suggestion to "be secure." It's a specific list of requirements that the FTC will audit and enforce. Here's what you need:

**A qualified individual overseeing your security program.** Someone (internal or outsourced) must be designated as responsible for your information security program. They must report to your board or senior leadership at least annually.

**A written risk assessment.** You must identify and assess risks to customer information across your entire operation. This isn't a one-time exercise. It needs to be updated as your business and threat landscape change.

**Access controls.** Limit who can access customer financial information. Not every employee needs access to every customer record. Implement role-based access and review permissions regularly.

**Encryption.** Customer data must be encrypted both in transit and at rest. If customer Social Security numbers, financial data, or credit applications are sitting on an unencrypted hard drive or being emailed in plain text, you're in violation.

**Multi-factor authentication.** MFA is required for anyone accessing customer information. No exceptions, no workarounds, no "but our DMS doesn't support it."

**Monitoring and logging.** You must implement tools to detect unauthorized access or activity. This means actual logging and monitoring, not just hoping nobody gets in.

**Security awareness training.** Your staff must be trained on security risks and your policies. Annual training at minimum, with additional training when threats or procedures change.

**Vendor management.** If third parties have access to customer data (and in a dealership, they always do, think DMS providers, F&I software, CRM systems), you must assess their security and contractually require them to safeguard the data.

**Incident response plan.** A written plan for how you'll detect, respond to, and recover from security events.

## The May 2024 reporting requirement

As of May 2024, if a breach affects 500 or more people, you must report it to the FTC within 30 days. This is separate from any state breach notification requirements (like the Kansas law discussed in my other post). The FTC reporting requirement is on top of whatever Kansas requires.

## What non-compliance costs

The FTC is not messing around with enforcement:

- **Companies:** Fines up to $100,000 per violation. Each customer record, each missing control, each day of non-compliance can be treated as a separate violation.
- **Auto dealerships specifically:** Fines of up to $11,000 per day that add up fast.
- **Individual liability:** The FTC can pursue the individuals responsible, not just the company. If you're the owner or the designated security person and you ignored the requirements, that's personal liability.

And beyond the fines, there's the business damage. A publicized FTC enforcement action destroys customer trust. Wichita is a relationship-driven market. If your customers find out their SSN and financial data were sitting unprotected on a shared drive, they're not coming back.

## The dealership-specific challenges

Auto dealerships have some unique security headaches that make compliance harder:

**Massive amounts of sensitive data.** Every credit application includes names, SSNs, income information, employer details, and bank account numbers. A busy dealership processes hundreds of these per month.

**High employee turnover.** Sales staff come and go. Every departure is a risk if access isn't promptly revoked and devices aren't properly wiped.

**Multiple third-party systems.** Your DMS (Reynolds, CDK), CRM, F&I tools, lender portals, and inventory systems all touch customer data. Each one is a potential weak point.

**Paper still everywhere.** Despite the digital systems, many dealerships still have paper credit apps, printed deal jackets, and physical files with customer data. Physical security matters too.

**Culture.** Let's be honest. Cybersecurity hasn't been a priority in automotive retail. The culture is "sell cars and make deals," and security gets treated as an obstacle. That attitude has to change because the FTC doesn't care about your culture.

## What a compliant program looks like

For a Wichita dealership, a practical Safeguards Rule compliance program looks like this:

1. **Designate your qualified individual.** This can be an outsourced IT/security provider. You don't need a full-time CISO for a two-rooftop dealership.
2. **Conduct the risk assessment.** Identify every system that touches customer data, assess the risks, and document everything.
3. **Implement technical controls.** Encryption, MFA, access controls, monitoring, endpoint protection. This is where the IT work happens.
4. **Write your policies.** Information security policy, incident response plan, vendor management procedures, acceptable use policy. These need to be real documents that people actually follow, not templates gathering dust.
5. **Train your people.** Annual security awareness training for all employees. Additional training for anyone handling customer financial data.
6. **Test and audit.** Regular vulnerability scans, penetration testing, and policy reviews. Document everything.
7. **Report annually.** Your qualified individual produces a written report for leadership covering the state of the program, risks, and recommendations.

## This applies beyond dealerships

While I've focused on auto dealers because that's a big industry in Wichita, remember that the Safeguards Rule applies to any "financial institution" under the GLBA definition. If you're a tax preparer, CPA, financial advisor, mortgage broker, or title company, the same requirements apply to you. The scope might be smaller, but the obligations are the same.
